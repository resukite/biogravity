The Mpemba Effect: Why Hot Water Can Freeze

Scientific Abstract

Discovered empirically in 1963 by Tanzanian high schooler Erasto Mpemba, the phenomenon where initially hot water freezes into ice faster than cold water baffled physicists from Aristotle to modern laboratories. Contemporary molecular dynamics experiments reveal that anomalous hydrogen bonding relaxation, evaporative convection loops, and dissolved gas expulsion drive this non-equilibrium thermodynamic anomaly.

Student & Researcher Key Takeaways
  • The Mpemba effect does not violate thermodynamics because the two bodies of water follow fundamentally different thermal trajectories through non-equilibrium phase space.
  • Evaporative mass reduction can eliminate up to 10–15% of the total water volume, meaning the hot container has less total liquid to freeze.
  • Strong convective Rayleigh-Bénard cells form in hot water, transporting core heat to the cold boundary layer much faster than conduction alone.
  • Heating water expels dissolved gases (oxygen, nitrogen) which otherwise lower freezing nucleation efficiency.
  • Synchrotron X-ray studies confirm hydrogen bond relaxation stores non-linear kinetic memory in liquid water.

1. The High School Student Who Challenged Modern Physics

In 1963, a Tanzanian secondary school student named Erasto Barthlomeo Mpemba was making ice cream with his classmates. Fearing that freezer space would run out, Mpemba skipped the standard cooling step and placed his boiled, sugary milk mixture directly into the freezer alongside other students' pre-cooled mixtures. When he opened the freezer an hour and a half later, his hot mixture had frozen solid, while the cold mixtures remained thick, slushy liquids.

When Mpemba asked his physics teacher why hot liquid could freeze faster than cold liquid, the teacher mocked him, declaring it "Mpemba's physics." Undeterred, Mpemba repeated the experiment with pure water and confronted visiting British physicist Denis G. Osborne from University College Dar es Salaam. Osborne verified the results in his own laboratory, publishing their landmark paper in Physics Education in 1969.

2. The Four Primary Physical Mechanisms

Classical intuition assumes that as hot water (e.g., at 80°C) cools down, it must pass through the state of cold water (e.g., at 20°C) before reaching 0°C, making it theoretically impossible for the hot sample to win the race. However, this assumption relies on quasi-static equilibrium—the false premise that temperature is uniform throughout the liquid at every microsecond. Real cooling is violent, turbulent, and non-uniform.

  • Evaporation & Mass Reduction: Water at 80°C evaporates rapidly into a dry freezer atmosphere. By the time it nears freezing temperatures, the hot vessel may have lost 8% to 15% of its total water mass. Cooling less mass requires less total enthalpy extraction ($Q = m c \Delta T$).
  • Violent Rayleigh-Bénard Convection: Hot water generates powerful buoyant convection currents. Cold water sinks along the container walls while hot water rushes to the surface, creating an efficient liquid radiator. In cold water, convection is sluggish, relying largely on slow thermal conduction.
  • Degassing & Supercooling Inhibition: Boiling or near-boiling water expels dissolved atmospheric gases ($O_2, N_2, CO_2$). Dissolved micro-bubbles in unheated cold water can inhibit uniform crystal nucleation and promote severe supercooling (where water drops to -4°C or -6°C without freezing).
  • Molecular Hydrogen Bond Relaxation: Work by chemical physicists at Nanyang Technological University demonstrates that heating stretches intermolecular $O:H-O$ hydrogen bonds while shortening the intramolecular covalent $O-H$ bonds. Upon rapid chilling, these stored conformational strain energies release rapidly, snapping water molecules into tetrahedral ice structures faster.

3. Mathematical Modeling of Non-Equilibrium Heat Flux

The rate of heat loss from a water container placed in a cold reservoir is governed by coupled convective, evaporative, and radiative cooling:

$$\frac{dQ}{dt} = - \left[ h A (T_s - T_{\infty}) + \epsilon \sigma A (T_s^4 - T_{\infty}^4) + \dot{m}_{\text{evap}} L_v \right]$$

Where $T_s$ is surface temperature, $T_{\infty}$ is ambient temperature, $h$ is the convective heat transfer coefficient, and $\dot{m}_{\text{evap}} L_v$ represents the massive latent heat carried away by vapor. Because evaporation scales exponentially with surface vapor pressure via the Clausius-Clapeyron relation:

$$P_{\text{sat}}(T) = P_0 \exp\left( -\frac{\Delta H_{\text{vap}}}{R} \left( \frac{1}{T} - \frac{1}{T_0} \right) \right)$$

A sample starting at 80°C expels latent heat at a rate more than 20 times higher during its initial minutes than a sample starting at 20°C, building momentum in thermal velocity gradients that carry it across the freezing threshold first.

Google Search & Featured Snippets FAQ

Does hot water always freeze faster than cold water? ▾

No. The Mpemba effect is sensitive to initial conditions, container geometry, freezer airflow, container conductivity, and dissolved mineral content. When open containers allow high evaporation and strong convection, the effect is pronounced; in tightly sealed, insulated containers, cold water often freezes first.

Does the Mpemba effect violate the laws of thermodynamics? ▾

No. Thermodynamics applies strictly to closed systems in static equilibrium. Cooling water is an open, non-equilibrium system undergoing simultaneous phase change, mass loss, and turbulent thermal transport, which obeys the conservation of energy perfectly.

How can I replicate the Mpemba effect at home? ▾

Use two identical wide-rimmed glass or thin metal cups. Fill one with 100 mL of freshly boiled water (around 85°C) and the other with 100 mL of room-temperature tap water (20°C). Place both side-by-side in a well-ventilated, frost-free deep freezer and inspect every 10 minutes.
