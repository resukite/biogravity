Core Finding & Direct Answer (TL;DR)

The Demon Core was a 6.2-kilogram plutonium sphere developed during the Manhattan Project that became famous after two fatal accidents in 1945 and 1946. While testing neutron reflectors to measure how close the core was to criticality, scientists accidentally enclosed it too tightly with tungsten carbide and beryllium, causing an instantaneous chain reaction of lethal neutron and gamma radiation.

Editorial Synopsis & Exploration

Manufactured at Los Alamos for a planned third atomic strike in World War II, the Demon Core was a 6.2-kilogram sphere of subcritical delta-phase plutonium-gallium alloy. In 1945 and 1946, two catastrophic human errors during experimental neutron reflection assemblies triggered prompt criticality accidents, bathing physicists Harry Daghlian and Louis Slotin in lethal neutron and gamma radiation bursts.

1. The Third Bomb and the Delta-Phase Sphere

In mid-August 1945, after the detonation of atomic weapons over Hiroshima and Nagasaki, technicians at the Manhattan Project's Los Alamos laboratory prepared a third plutonium pit for a scheduled drop over Tokyo. Following Japan's unconditional surrender on August 15, the core was retained at the Omega Site in Los Alamos for critical mass experimentation. Technicians nicknamed it 'Rufus'; after claiming two lives in ten months, it was forever re-christened The Demon Core.

Metallurgically, the core consisted of two solid hemispheres of plutonium-239 stabilized in the malleable delta phase with approximately 1% gallium by weight, clad in a thin protective layer of nickel to prevent corrosion and alpha particle contamination. The sphere had a mass of 6.2 kilograms (13.7 pounds) and measured just 89 millimeters (3.5 inches) in diameter—small enough to sit in the palm of a hand, yet dense enough to sink like a cannonball.

2. The Physics of Neutron Multiplication and Criticality

In a subcritical mass of plutonium, spontaneous fissions release energetic neutrons, but more than 95% escape through the spherical surface area before striking another plutonium nucleus. To initiate an explosive or self-sustaining reaction, physicists surround the core with a neutron reflector (such as tungsten carbide or beryllium). Reflected neutrons bounce back into the fissile material, dramatically reducing the critical mass threshold:

$$k_{\text{eff}} = \frac{\text{neutrons produced in generation } n}{\text{neutrons lost (absorption + leakage) in generation } n - 1}$$

When $k_{\text{eff}} < 1$, the assembly is subcritical. When $k_{\text{eff}} = 1.0$, it is critical (steady state). But if $k_{\text{eff}} > 1.0$ on prompt neutrons alone (without waiting for delayed neutrons from fission fragments), the system enters prompt supercriticality: the neutron population escalates exponentially on a microsecond timescale:

$$N(t) = N_0 \exp\left( \frac{k_{\text{eff}} - 1}{\ell} \cdot t \right)$$

Where $\ell \approx 10^{-8}$ seconds is the prompt neutron lifetime in metallic plutonium. If $k_{\text{eff}}$ exceeds 1.002, the fission rate multiplies by billions within a fraction of a millisecond, liberating a tremendous flash of gamma rays and neutrons before thermal expansion blows the parts apart.

3. The Two Fatal Encounters: Daghlian and Slotin

The First Accident (August 21, 1945): 24-year-old physicist Harry Daghlian was working alone late at night building a wall of 4.4 kg tungsten carbide bricks around the plutonium sphere. As he was placing the final brick over the top of the core, his neutron counters registered that the assembly was about to go critical. As he attempted to pull his hand back, the heavy brick slipped from his fingers and plunged directly onto the center of the sphere. The core instantly reached prompt criticality. Daghlian reacted instinctively, knocking the brick away with his bare hand, but in those seconds he absorbed an estimated 510 rem (5.1 Sv) of radiation. He died 25 days later.

The Second Accident (May 21, 1946): 35-year-old physicist Louis Slotin was demonstrating a criticality experiment to seven colleagues. Slotin placed the lower half of the Demon Core inside a hemispherical shell of beryllium and lowered a matching upper beryllium dome over it. To prevent the dome from completely enclosing the core—which would cause instant criticality—standard protocols required using shims. Slotin, known for his reckless bravado, used the tip of a flathead screwdriver to hold the dome ajar. The screwdriver slipped.

Eyewitness Description of the Flash

"The instant the upper hemisphere seated, a blinding blue glow illuminated the laboratory and a wave of heat swept through the air. Slotin experienced a sour metallic taste in his mouth from ionized nerve endings on his tongue. He flipped the upper dome off with his bare hand to shield his companions, but his dose was fatal."

Slotin absorbed roughly 1,000 rad (10 Gy) of neutron and gamma radiation. Despite prompt medical attention, his gastrointestinal tract and bone marrow suffered total destruction, and he succumbed nine days later.

Google Search & Featured Snippets FAQ

What was the Demon Core made of? ▾

The Demon Core was a 6.2 kg (14 lb) subcritical sphere of delta-phase plutonium-gallium alloy with a diameter of approximately 8.9 centimeters (3.5 inches).

What caused the blue flash in the Demon Core accidents? ▾

The blue flash was Cherenkov radiation caused by high-energy neutrons and gamma rays exciting nitrogen and oxygen molecules in the ambient air, generating a characteristic blue glow of ionized air.

What happened to the Demon Core after the accidents? ▾

Following Louis Slotin's fatal accident in 1946, the Demon Core was melted down in the summer of 1946, and its plutonium was re-purposed into other nuclear weapon cores.
