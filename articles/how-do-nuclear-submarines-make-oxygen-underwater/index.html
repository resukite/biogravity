Core Finding & Direct Answer (TL;DR)

Nuclear submarines make oxygen by desalinating ocean water into pure distilled water and running an electrical current through it via electrolysis ($2\text{H}_2\text{O} \to 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$). The oxygen is pumped into the air ducts, while explosive hydrogen is pumped overboard. Carbon dioxide is removed using regenerative monoethanolamine (MEA) scrubbers.

Student & Researcher Key Takeaways
  • ✓ Nuclear submarines generate breathable oxygen by splitting ocean water using high-pressure electrolysis: 2H2O + electrical energy -> 2H2 + O2.
  • ✓ Seawater is first desalinated via reverse osmosis and deionized into ultra-pure distilled water so harmful chlorine gas is not released during electrolysis.
  • ✓ The nuclear reactor provides limitless electrical power to run the electrolysis units, while dangerous byproduct hydrogen gas is safely vented into the ocean.
  • ✓ Carbon dioxide exhaled by the crew is continuously scrubbed from the air using regenerative liquid Monoethanolamine (MEA) chemical absorption towers.

11. The Primary Engine: High-Pressure Water Electrolysis

A modern ballistic missile or fast-attack nuclear submarine (such as the US Navy's Virginia or Ohio class) carries a crew of 130 to 160 sailors and can remain submerged for months at a time, limited only by food rations.

To keep 140 people alive, the life-support system must generate approximately 400 to 500 liters of pure molecular oxygen per hour. This is handled by automated electrolytic oxygen generators (such as the Low-Pressure Electrolyzer or OOG system).

Submarines never electrolyze raw seawater directly, as the chloride ions ($Cl^-$) in ocean salt would oxidize into lethal, corrosive chlorine gas ($Cl_2$). Instead, raw ocean water is processed through multi-stage flash distillation and reverse osmosis to achieve deionized water ($18\text{ M}\Omega\cdot\text{cm}$) before being fed into the proton exchange membrane (PEM) electrolysis cell:

$$2\text{H}_2\text{O}_{(l)} + 4e^- \longrightarrow 2\text{H}_{2(g)} \uparrow + \text{O}_{2(g)} \uparrow \quad (E^0 = -1.23\text{ V})$$

22. The Silent Killer: Scrubbing Carbon Dioxide

Generating oxygen is only half the life-support battle. In a closed submarine hull, elevated carbon dioxide ($CO_2$) is far more dangerous than low oxygen. Exhaled air contains roughly 40,000 ppm $CO_2$. If ambient concentrations exceed 10,000 ppm (1.0%), crew members suffer headaches, lethargy, cognitive impairment, and acidosis.

Submarines use Monoethanolamine (MEA) scrubbers. Ambient cabin air is blown through a column of chilled liquid MEA, which chemically binds $CO_2$. The enriched MEA solution is then pumped into a secondary boiler heated by reactor steam: heat reverses the chemical reaction, releasing pure concentrated $CO_2$ gas which is discharged overboard into the seawater, while the rejuvenated MEA is recycled back to the scrubber.

Google Search & Featured Snippets FAQ

What happens to the hydrogen gas produced during electrolysis? ▾

Hydrogen ($H_2$) gas is extremely flammable and explosive if allowed to accumulate above 4% in air. Submarines use high-pressure compressors to pump the hydrogen overboard into the surrounding ocean, where it dissolves harmlessly.

What are submarine oxygen candles? ▾

If the reactor fails or electrical power is lost, submarines carry emergency chemical oxygen generators nicknamed 'candles'. Made of sodium chlorate ($NaClO_3$) and iron powder, lighting an oxygen candle triggers an exothermic thermal decomposition that produces pure breathable oxygen for several hours without electricity.

Why can't diesel submarines stay submerged as long as nuclear submarines? ▾

Diesel engines require vast amounts of oxygen to combust fuel, requiring diesel-electric submarines to frequently surface or extend a snorkel mast. Nuclear reactors require no oxygen at all, generating endless electricity and steam while remaining fully sealed thousands of feet beneath the surface.
